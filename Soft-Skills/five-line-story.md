# The 5-Line Story Method

Based on Tim Runia's framework: before writing a script, shot list, or outline, reduce the entire story to exactly five lines. These five lines are the emotional core — the "life thread" — of the story. If you can't write them, you don't have a story yet; no editing trick, camera trick, or clever formatting will save it.

## The core problem this solves

Storytelling fails most often from **losing the core in the chaos of decision-making** — gear, transitions, voiceovers, color grading, slide design, word choice. The 5 lines let you see the whole story *before* the details distract you, and give you something to return to whenever the project gets overwhelming.

## The 5 Lines

Write exactly one line for each. One sentence per line — no cheating with run-ons.

| # | Line | Question it answers | Why it matters to the audience |
|---|------|--------------------|-------------------------------|
| 1 | **Situation** | What's the starting point? Where are we, what's happening, who's in the story? | Gives the viewer a clear anchor — the world we're in and what this is about |
| 2 | **Desire** | What does the character want? | Sparks curiosity; the audience leans in and starts to care whether they get it. Often it's something the audience wants too |
| 3 | **Conflict** | What gets in the way? | Tension is non-negotiable — without an obstacle there's no reason to keep watching |
| 4 | **Change** | What happens that shifts something? | The turning point — an insight, action, or new way of seeing. Shows growth and forward movement |
| 5 | **Result** | How does it end? What's the new reality? | Delivers satisfaction and closure; ties everything together and shows how things differ from line 1 |

### Worked example — The Lion King

1. **Situation:** Simba is a young prince excited to one day rule the Pride Lands.
2. **Desire:** He wants to prove he's brave and worthy of being king.
3. **Conflict:** After his father's death, Simba runs away, ashamed and afraid to face his past.
4. **Change:** Grown up, with help from friends and a vision of his father, Simba returns to confront Scar.
5. **Result:** He restores balance to the Pride Lands and steps fully into who he was meant to be.

Details are missing — but it's the *whole story*. Almost every movie, talk, and viral video follows this shape; that's how humans naturally process stories.

### Worked example — a personal/creator story

1. **Situation:** I'm a filmmaker who has loved making videos since I was a kid.
2. **Desire:** My dream was to be a YouTuber, sharing stories from the heart — not just for clients.
3. **Conflict:** Every time I tried to start, self-doubt and overthinking pulled me back.
4. **Change:** When I let go of the dream, I realized I'd been focused on the wrong reasons — I just wanted to create stories.
5. **Result:** I finally sat down and started; this video is the result, and that first step pushed me all in.

## Expanding: 5 → 10 → 20 → full script

The 5 lines are a **launchpad, not the finished piece**. To expand:

- Split each line into 2 (then 4) lines, adding specifics, dialogue, sensory detail, and stakes — e.g. "self-doubt pulled me back" becomes the actual excuses: *"I'm too late, too old, I don't have the skills, the gear, the security."*
- Expanding never changes the core — it only adds richness. Keep going until the 5 lines have become the whole script.
- **The connect-back test:** every scene, slide, or paragraph you add must trace back to one of the 5 lines. If it doesn't connect to any line, it's a detail that will make the audience "lose the plot" — cut it.

## How to apply this skill

**When writing a story from scratch:**
1. Ask (or infer) the subject, audience, and medium.
2. Draft the 5 lines first and show them to the user before writing anything longer.
3. Once the 5 lines are approved, expand to the target length, keeping the line structure visible (or noting which section serves which line).

**When reviewing/fixing a draft:**
1. Reverse-engineer the draft into its 5 lines.
2. Diagnose by absence: no clear Situation → audience is disoriented; no Desire → nobody cares; no Conflict → no reason to keep watching/reading; no Change → the story feels static; no Result → it ends without closure.
3. Flag material that connects to none of the 5 lines as cuttable.

**When the user is overwhelmed or stuck mid-project:**
Bring them back to their 5 lines. Check each piece they're building against the lines — this is the method's recovery mechanism, not just its planning step.

## Principles to enforce

- **Simple is good.** Resist the urge to complicate the structure before the core is nailed.
- **Core before craft.** Never let formatting, visuals, or polish decisions start before the 5 lines exist.
- **One sentence per line.** The constraint is the point — it forces clarity about what the story is actually about.
- **Emotion is the goal.** The blueprint exists to make people *feel* something; check that the Desire and Conflict lines carry real stakes.

## When the 5 lines won't come

If the user can't produce five lines — or you can't from the material they've given you — that *is* the finding, not a blocker. Usually one specific line is missing, and which one tells you what's actually wrong:

- **No Situation** → they haven't decided who this is for or where it starts.
- **No Desire** → there's a topic but nobody wanting anything. Most "my content is boring" problems live here.
- **No Conflict** → they're describing a process, not a story. Ask what went wrong or what was hard.
- **No Change** → they have a before and an after but no hinge. Ask what specifically caused the shift.
- **No Result** → either the project isn't finished, or they don't yet know what it meant.

Name the missing line and ask the single question that would produce it. That's more useful than a full draft built on a guess.

## Don't invent the user's story

Resist completing the set yourself. A fabricated Conflict is the most damaging possible error here, because every later line and every scene you expand inherits its direction — the user ends up with a polished script about something that didn't happen, and finding that out costs more than the draft saved.

When the story is theirs, ask rather than fill in. If you need to show the shape before they've supplied details, write the line as a visible gap (`3. **Conflict:** [what actually got in the way?]`) or offer two or three candidate lines their material could support and let them pick the true one. Recognising the right line is easy for them; auditing your invention is not.

## Output format

**Writing from scratch:** deliver the five lines as a labelled numbered list, one sentence each, and stop there for confirmation before expanding. A wrong spine is cheap to fix at five lines and expensive to fix at five hundred words.

**Reviewing a draft:** reverse-engineer the five lines from what they wrote, noting which part of the draft each came from, then name the missing or weak line. List the material connecting to no line as cuttable — with rough volume, since "roughly a third of this connects to nothing" lands harder than a general note about tightening.

**Expanding:** keep visible markers showing which line each section serves (e.g. `[Conflict]`). Seeing the spine survive the detail is the entire point of the method, and it gives the user something to check their own additions against after you're gone.

## Related skills

Four storytelling skills in this collection divide the work rather than compete:

- **5-Line Story** (this one) — the structural spine. Reach for it when there's no story yet, or the draft is shapeless.
- **Dopamine Ladder** (`dopamine-ladder.md`) — retention engineering. Reach for it when the structure is sound but attention leaks.
- **Three Ingredients** (`three-ingredients.md`) — texture, delivery, and meaning. Reach for it when the story is structurally fine but flat on the page or in the mouth.
- **Aristotle's Five Elements** (`aristotle-five-elements.md`) — persuading with facts. Reach for it when the raw material is information rather than lived experience: analysis, briefings, launches, positioning, commentary.

Building a script from scratch, the usual order is 5-Line Story → Dopamine Ladder → Three Ingredients: spine, then retention, then texture. One distinction worth keeping straight — the **Result** line is what changed *in the story*, which is not the same as the *meaning* (what it changes for the listener, covered in Three Ingredients). A story can land a satisfying Result and still leave the audience with nothing that applies to them. Aristotle's Five Elements sits on a separate track — information-heavy writing rather than narrative — so reach for that one when the material is facts that have to persuade.
