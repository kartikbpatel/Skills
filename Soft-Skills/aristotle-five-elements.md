# Storytelling: Aristotle's Five Elements

A playbook for the case where you are not short of material — you are short of a reason for anyone to spend time on it. Most information-heavy writing fails not because the facts are wrong but because the facts arrive with no authority, no feeling, nothing to hold on to, and no end in sight.

Palki Sharma Upadhyay built India's first international prime-time news show, at WION, around these ideas after concluding that every channel had the same facts, the same guests, and the same graphics — so the only remaining variable was the telling.

## The premise: katha and kathakar

Children ask for the same bedtime story again and again. They know the ending. What pulls them back is not the *katha* — the story — but the *kathakar*, the teller.

This is the whole basis of the skill. When the facts are commodity (and in a market, a news cycle, or an industry, they almost always are), differentiation lives entirely in the telling. Two people with identical data produce a memo nobody finishes and a memo that changes a decision. So the useful question is almost never "do I have enough information?" It is "why would anyone choose my version of this?"

The stakes are higher than style. Palki's example: the US had Kyoto on its atom-bomb target list in 1945. Secretary of War Henry Stimson struck it off — he had honeymooned there and had seen the city's beauty for himself. Nagasaki went on the list instead. Kyoto's story saved it. Whoever tells a story defines the thing being told about; if you leave yours untold, someone else's lens fills the gap, and you will spend your energy reacting to their framing instead of setting your own.

## Two modes

**Build** — the user wants a piece written. Work through *Before you write* (below), then draft with the five elements as construction material, not decoration.

**Review** — the user has a draft, or says something isn't landing. Score the five, name the single weakest, and rewrite that part. Do not sprinkle small improvements across all five; a piece with one broken element reads as broken, and fixing the break is what changes the outcome.

## Before you write: three questions

These take two minutes and they determine whether the draft has anywhere to go. Skipping them is why competent writing comes out generic.

**1. Whose lens is currently on this subject, and what does it get wrong?**

Palki's first gap: a country of 1.4 billion with hundreds of channels, whose own image was set by the *New York Times*, the *Economist*, the BBC. Not because those outlets lied, but because they were the only ones telling it, through their own priors.

Ask the equivalent for your subject. Who is defining it now — a competitor's marketing, an analyst report, a loud thread, an internal assumption nobody has revisited? Name the specific distortion in their framing. That distortion is your opening, because it is the thing your reader half-believes and cannot yet articulate.

**2. Where is the gap?**

Palki structures her own talk as a sequence of gaps she noticed and tried to fill. It is a good habit because a gap is inherently interesting — it is a hole in the reader's picture of the world, and holes create pull. A claim does not.

"Here is what we shipped" has no gap. "Every tool in this category assumes your data is clean; nobody's is" has one. Find the thing that is missing, unasked, or assumed — and lead with it.

**3. What is your flavor?**

An editor told Palki: don't do *water journalism* — colorless, flavorless, odorless. This is the age of Coke. Find your flavor.

Flavor is a specific editorial promise, not a tone. Hers was: **inform without making a fuss; give the reader value for time.** Yours might be "no claim without a number," or "always tells you what would change our mind," or "the thing your own team won't say out loud." Pick one and let it govern every cut. Without it, you will default to whatever the category already sounds like.

## The five elements

Aristotle's list as Palki uses it. Worth knowing the provenance before citing it to anyone who has studied rhetoric: the canonical appeals are only ethos, logos, and pathos. Metaphor and brevity come from Aristotle's treatment of *style* in Rhetoric III rather than from that triad, and where classical rhetoric adds a fourth term it is usually kairos, timing. The five-element synthesis is hers and it is a good one — just not a canonical list, and overclaiming the pedigree would cost exactly the ethos the framework is built on. Read them as five distinct loads a piece has to carry. When something isn't working, one of these is missing, and it is usually identifiable in seconds.

### Ethos — authority and character

Do people trust you, and do they see you as having standing on this subject? If yes, they will listen; if no, nothing downstream matters, because a reader who does not trust the source discounts every number in the piece.

Ethos is earned in the text, not asserted by job title. What earns it: showing your working, citing sources a skeptic could check, being specific about method and sample, and — the one most writers skip — naming the limits of your own claim. Stating what your data cannot show is the single cheapest, strongest credibility move available, because it signals you are describing reality rather than selling.

*Broken when:* the piece makes confident assertions with no visible basis, hides the sample size, or claims a category-defining insight from three customer calls.

### Logos — reason

The logical spine: data and facts assembled into an argument, because you cannot make an assertion with no basis in fact or logic.

Practical test: every load-bearing claim should have a number, a named source, or a mechanism attached. Not every sentence — every claim that the conclusion depends on. If you remove the adjectives and the argument collapses, there was no logos.

*Broken when:* the piece runs on directional language ("significant," "growing rapidly," "increasingly," "best-in-class") that survives unchanged if the underlying facts reverse.

### Pathos — emotion, which is not drama

This is the distinction that does the most work in the whole framework, and the one to get right.

Palki is explicit: pathos is *a genuine connect with the audience through honest and effective communication*. Drama is decibel level — the shouting match, the twelve windows on screen when the competition has ten, "game-changing," "revolutionary," "we're thrilled to announce." Drama is what people reach for when they have no pathos, and readers detect the substitution immediately.

Real pathos in factual writing usually comes from one move: make the reader recognise a situation they are actually in. Not "efficiency gains" but the person on their team who will spend Friday reconciling two spreadsheets. Not "customer churn" but the account manager who found out from LinkedIn. Concrete, second-person, unexaggerated. The emotion arrives on its own; you do not have to name it, and naming it ("this is exciting") reliably kills it.

*Broken when:* the piece is either bloodless — abstractions and passive constructions all the way down, nobody in it — or hype-inflated, using intensity to simulate significance.

### Metaphor — a handle for the complex thing

A relatable parallel lets a reader process something they have no prior slot for, and it is what makes the point survive the walk to the next meeting. This is why one good analogy outperforms three paragraphs of accurate explanation.

Rules of thumb: one metaphor per complex idea, drawn from something physical and everyday, and it has to be structurally honest — the parallel should hold at the point you are using it for, or a sharp reader will use the mismatch to dismiss the argument. Mixed or decorative metaphors ("unlock the runway to a north-star flywheel") do the opposite of this job.

*Broken when:* a genuinely hard concept is explained only in its own vocabulary, so the reader who didn't already understand it still doesn't.

### Brevity — short sentences, punchy lines

Short sentences, clean lines, no hedge stacking. Brevity is not about total length — a 3,000-word piece can be brief and a 200-word one bloated. It is about the reader never having to re-read a sentence to find its subject.

Hedges are the usual culprit in business writing: *it seems that there may potentially be some indication that*. Each one is an attempt to avoid being wrong, and stacked they make the writer look less credible rather than more careful — so brevity is doing ethos work too. If a claim needs a caveat, state the claim in one sentence and the caveat in the next.

*Broken when:* sentences run past forty words, the main verb is buried, or you can delete a third of the words without losing information.

### Using them as a diagnostic

Score the draft 1–5 on each, then fix the lowest. Do not average them — a piece with strong logos and zero pathos does not read as "average," it reads as unreadable. The five have a rough order of operations: **ethos and logos earn the right to be heard; pathos and metaphor make it stick; brevity makes it survive the reader's inbox.** A piece missing one of the first two cannot be rescued by the last three.

## When everything sounds the same: orbit shift

Reach for this when the user's real problem is sameness — "our content is indistinguishable," "the deck is fine but forgettable," "we said we'd do something different and produced the same thing."

Palki's diagnosis: you are held down by cumulative gravity. Gravity keeps you grounded, and it also stops you flying. It comes in four layers, and the value of separating them is that each has a different escape route — misdiagnosing the layer is why "let's be bolder" never works.

1. **Personal gravity** — *this is what I can do, this is what I cannot.* Self-doubt and the limits your own mind sets. Escape: find one person who has done the version you think is impossible.
2. **Company gravity** — *this cannot be done here; we won't get cross-functional support.* Often a rule inherited from a constraint that no longer exists. Escape: ask when the rule was set and who would actually object today.
3. **Industry gravity** — *how can you do a 9 p.m. show without guests? Nobody does that.* The strongest layer, because it is invisible: it looks like professionalism. Escape: list the format conventions everyone in your category follows, then ask which serve the audience and which serve the producer.
4. **Cultural gravity** — prejudice and preconception. Palki's example is India's fondness for *jugaad*, which quietly licenses the absence of a long-term plan. Escape: name the belief out loud; it usually cannot survive being stated plainly.

Cumulative gravity kills ideas, and — this is the part worth transmitting to the user — you rationalise all of it in the name of practicality. That is what it feels like from the inside: not cowardice, but good judgement. Which is why *sometimes you have to be impractical, throw yourself at the deep end and burn the bridge to safety.* Breaking out of the orbit is the **orbit shift**.

The result is usually less exotic than the effort suggests. Palki's show dropped the panel of guests and spent the time on tight scripts, research, and fact-checks — "basic things really, which should ideally be SOP for all news, but they were not." Expect orbit shifts to look like doing an obvious thing that the category's gravity had made unthinkable.

**Diagnostic question when a draft is generic:** if the only thing separating this from a competitor's version is volume, adjectives, or production value, you are in the copy-of-a-copy trap. *If you have ten windows, I'll have twelve* is not differentiation. Ask what the reader actually wants — a question that gets skipped precisely because everyone is watching each other.

## Purpose: the through-line

Use this for talks, keynotes, all-hands, personal narrative, or any time the user has good material and no spine.

"Where do you see yourself in five years" is unanswerable, and Palki says plainly that she cannot answer it. So she swaps it for a question that actually has an answer:

> **What is the one story that stayed with you?**

Hers is the ugly duckling: the odd one out, miserable about not fitting in, who sees a flock of swans and realises he was never a duck to begin with. She keeps it because it is simple, relatable, and it *talks to her* — everyone has been the ugly duckling under pressure to fit in and blaming themselves for failing.

The mechanism is worth understanding rather than copying: the formative story is not autobiography, it is a lens. Find it and the through-line falls out of it. Hers ran: stories shape people → mass media is where people find stories → so my purpose is to find and tell the ones that trigger ideas and conversations, and to leave the conclusion to an audience intelligent enough to choose.

Two things to hold onto when using this. The story does not need to be impressive — a nursery tale did the job here; the criterion is that it is *true for the person telling it*. And the purpose is stated as service to the audience, not as ambition. "Empower you to form your own opinion" is a promise to the room; "become the most trusted voice in news" would have been a promise to herself, and rooms can tell the difference.

Related opening move, useful for any talk about a career or a choice: **will your story be more exciting than your CV?** Woke up, went to work, hit targets, got promoted, didn't drop many balls, didn't disrupt much either. Ask it, then let the silence do the work.

## Don't manufacture the logos

This framework asks every load-bearing claim to carry a number, and the worked example below does exactly that — five specific figures appearing in a rewrite. Those figures are invented, because the example is demonstrating a shape. Doing it to a real draft is the one failure here that turns good writing into a liability: the user forwards it, someone asks where 60% came from, and the answer is nowhere.

So treat logos as an input you have to be given, not one you can generate. When a draft has no numbers in it, the finding is *"there is no logos here and I can't supply it"* — followed by the specific list of figures the argument would need. That list is the valuable part; it tells the user what to go measure. A rewrite resting on plausible placeholders looks finished instead, which is worse, because nobody audits a finished draft.

If you need to show the rewritten shape before the data exists, mark every invented figure visibly — `[% of pilot accounts]`, `[time-to-touch, before → after]` — and say in the preamble that the numbers are placeholders. The same applies to the ethos move of naming your own limits: don't invent a caveat like a 500-deal threshold, ask what the real constraint is. A fabricated limitation is worse than none at all, because it reads as candour while being fiction.

## Output format

**Review:** score the five elements 1–5 with a one-line reason each, name the weakest, rewrite that part, then say what changed. The worked example below is the template. Scoring all five is worth the space because it shows which parts were already working — otherwise a rewrite lands as a verdict on the whole piece.

**Build:** answer the three pre-writing questions briefly, then the draft, then a short note on which element each choice was serving. That note is what lets the user apply the framework themselves next time rather than coming back for another rewrite.

Match depth to the ask. A one-paragraph LinkedIn post doesn't need a five-element scorecard, and producing one anyway is the exact kind of fuss this skill argues against.

## Worked example

*The figures in the rewrite are invented to show the shape — see "Don't manufacture the logos" above before doing this to a real draft.*

**Draft as received:**

> We are excited to announce that our new AI-powered lead scoring engine is now generally available. This game-changing capability leverages advanced machine learning to revolutionize how sales teams prioritize their pipeline, delivering unprecedented efficiency gains and empowering organizations to unlock their full revenue potential.

**Diagnosis:** ethos 1 (no reason to believe any of it), logos 1 (not one number), pathos 1 — and note it is not *absent*, it is faked: "excited," "game-changing," "revolutionize," "unprecedented" are drama standing in for feeling. Metaphor 1, brevity 2. Weakest: logos and pathos together, which is the standard failure mode for launch copy.

**Rewritten:**

> Your reps call the wrong leads first.
>
> We measured it across 40 pilot accounts: reps worked their lists in arrival order, and 60% of the leads that eventually converted were sitting below the fold, untouched for three days or more.
>
> It's the queue at a busy clinic where everyone is seen in the order they walked in — including the person having a heart attack.
>
> Lead scoring reorders the queue. In the pilot, time-to-first-touch on high-intent leads went from 31 hours to 4. Conversion on that segment moved from 8% to 13%.
>
> It is not magic and it is not finished. The model needs roughly 500 closed deals in an account before its ranking beats arrival order, so new accounts should keep running their current process alongside it for the first quarter.

**What changed.** Logos: four specific numbers, each attached to a claim the conclusion rests on. Pathos: the reader's own rep, on a Friday, calling the wrong person — recognition, with no adjective doing the emotional work. Metaphor: triage, which is structurally honest, because triage really is reordering a queue by urgency rather than arrival. Brevity: the longest sentence is 28 words and the opener is six. Ethos: carried almost entirely by the final paragraph — the 500-deal threshold and the instruction to keep the old process running are what make the earlier numbers believable, because a seller would not have volunteered them.

## Related skills

Four storytelling skills in this collection divide the work rather than compete:

- **5-Line Story** (`five-line-story.md`) — the structural spine. Reach for it when there's no story yet, or the draft is shapeless.
- **Dopamine Ladder** (`dopamine-ladder.md`) — retention engineering. Reach for it when the structure is sound but attention leaks.
- **Three Ingredients** (`three-ingredients.md`) — texture, delivery, and meaning. Reach for it when the story is structurally fine but flat on the page or in the mouth.
- **Aristotle's Five Elements** (this one) — persuading with facts. Reach for it when the raw material is information rather than lived experience: analysis, briefings, launches, positioning, commentary.

The first three are the narrative track, used in that order for a script or a talk: spine, then retention, then texture. This one is the separate track for information-heavy writing. A keynote usually needs both — set the spine and purpose here, then craft the individual anecdotes inside it with Three Ingredients.

## Two habits worth keeping

**Value for time.** After any draft, ask what the reader received in the first fifteen seconds and whether the whole thing was worth the minutes it takes. This is the standard that produced Palki's flavor, and it cuts more effectively than any style rule.

**Inform without making a fuss.** Trust the reader. Palki's closing line — *because you're intelligent enough to choose* — is an editorial stance, not a pleasantry: give people the facts, the reasoning, and the limits, then let them reach the conclusion. Writing that insists on the conclusion is the one thing that reliably loses the audience it is trying to convince.
