# Small-Cap Thesis Discipline

What survives 30 years of managing other people's money is not a list of stocks — it is a set of tests that kill weak theses early and cheaply. This skill is those tests.

The framing throughout: a good company is not a thesis. "Is this a good business?" is nearly always answerable yes for anything worth discussing, which makes it a useless question. Every test below converts that into something that can actually come back no.

## Read this before applying it

**Separate the framework from the calls.** The source interview contains both durable method and time-stamped opinion. The method travels; the opinions have a shelf life measured in months.

| Real-return convergence | Whether large caps or small caps are currently cheap |

Holdings named in the source are illustrations of the framework at one date, not recommendations. When a user asks about one of them, apply the tests to current numbers rather than repeating the position.

**This is analysis, not advice.** The output is a structured thesis with its own kill-conditions — never a recommendation to buy or sell, and never sized to someone's personal finances. If a user wants a decision made for them, give them the framework and the specific things they'd need to check, and say plainly that the call is theirs.

## The core test: does it double in 3–4 years?

The deployment rule is that every rupee goes only into positions with a high probability of roughly doubling in 3–4 years. That is 18–24% compounded. If nothing clears the bar, hold cash — a genuinely absolute-return book will sit at 15%+ cash rather than deploy into something that fails the test.

Why this is the right primary filter: it forces price and time into the same sentence as quality. A 50x-earnings company growing 10% is a fine business and an automatic no, regardless of its index weight or how much everyone likes it.

**Strike rate is part of the design, not a failure of it.** Expect 5–7 of every 10 positions to work. That still blends to a high-teens portfolio return provided the winners are allowed to run and the losers were sized to lose small. A framework that needs 9 of 10 to work isn't a framework, it's a hope.

This is what makes a **granular book** rational rather than lazy: many positions, each sized so that being wrong is survivable, which in turn permits owning things with genuinely asymmetric payoffs. Lynch's Magellan record — roughly 29% a year over 13 years, run with well over a thousand names — is the reference case for granularity as a deliberate strategy rather than a lack of conviction.

## Horizon: why three years

Under one year, fundamentals don't drive the price; you are trading flows and sentiment with fundamental tools. Beyond five, the macro is unknowable — nobody can tell you where policy rates will be. Three years is where fundamental work actually has edge.

**The exit rule that matters most is broken versus deferred.** Getting this wrong in either direction is expensive:

- **Broken** — management isn't executing, or the business model assumption was wrong. Exit the moment you know, whether that's six months in or three years in. Time elapsed is irrelevant; the thesis is void.
- **Deferred** — the thesis was right and an external event pushed it out. A cyclical thesis interrupted in year two by a macro shock is a four-or-five-year thesis now, not a mistake. Extend it.

The discipline is asking which one you're in *before* looking at the P&L, because the P&L looks identical either way.

**Wrong for the right reason.** Sometimes the process was sound and an unknown unknown hit it. Leverage being a risk is taught in school; "being outdoors is a risk" was not, until 2020. The dangerous response is not the loss — it's retreating into a defensive shell afterwards, because that permanently degrades the ability to take the next good risk.

## Valuing something not yet profitable

Project profit three to four years out, apply an exit multiple you are willing to defend out loud, discount back, then run the doubling test on the result. Stating the multiple explicitly is the whole point — it's the assumption most likely to be wrong and the one a reader can argue with. A thesis that hides its exit multiple isn't falsifiable.

## Why a stock works — and why small caps differ

A position works for one of three reasons: **great management, great business model, or great valuation.** The practical question is where you can still find extremes of any of them.

In a large-cap universe, coverage is dense and price discovery efficient, so extremes are largely arbitraged away. In an under-covered universe of several hundred investable small caps, they are not — different segments sit in different parts of their own cycles, and genuine distress and genuine hyper-growth coexist.

This makes a common comparison a category error worth naming out loud: **the P/E of a small-cap index tells you very little about what a selective small-cap portfolio can do.** The index is the whole distribution; a concentrated-by-criteria portfolio is a deliberate tail of it. Someone arguing "small caps are expensive" from an index multiple is arguing about a thing nobody is required to buy.

The real constraint on small caps isn't valuation, it's deployment: you cannot put large sums to work in a handful of names, so the same capital requires many more decisions. That's a labour and process problem, not a returns ceiling.

## Consolidation, not market size

**GDP growth and equity returns are different things,** and conflating them is one of the most common errors in top-down investing. A fast-growing economy can deliver a flat market for years — China is the standard example. Agriculture has an enormous addressable market and produces almost no equity returns.

The reason is that **profit pools are created by consolidation.** An industry that grows while staying fragmented competes its returns away; the growth accrues to customers, not shareholders. So for any sector thesis, the question after "is it growing?" is "is it consolidating, consolidated, or fragmenting?" — and a fragmenting industry with a wonderful TAM is a trap.

## The industry-ROE signal

Here is the specific entry pattern worth learning: **when an entire industry's weighted-average return on equity sits below its cost of equity, that is often the window.**

The mechanism is that this state cannot persist. Whoever funds the sub-economic players eventually stops. Capacity exits, competition rationalises, the survivors' ROE rises above cost of equity, and the leader re-rates — often violently, because the market prices the distress right up until the consolidation is visible. Bharti through the entry-and-shakeout years is the canonical case: flat for a long stretch, then a multibagger once the industry structure resolved.

Two conditions make or break this trade:

1. **The timing is genuinely unknowable.** You cannot forecast when marginal players capitulate. This is the honest argument for a small position held patiently rather than a large one held anxiously — and for having several such positions, so that being early in one is survivable.
2. **Own the leader, not the cheapest.** The leader has the balance sheet, the people, and the distribution to still be there on the far side. The cheapest name is cheap because it may be the capacity that exits.

Diagnostic checklist: what share of the industry is loss-making or earning below cost of equity? Who is funding them, and what would make that funding stop? Is there a regulatory or solvency constraint that forces exit on a timetable? Who is unambiguously the leader?

## Where the marginal rupee goes

Past a threshold of per-capita income, the incremental unit of spending shifts away from staples toward healthcare, wellness, and protection. So a sector growing faster than nominal GDP is structurally more interesting than one growing with it — and the inflection can be a hockey stick rather than a ramp, because affordability crosses a line for a whole cohort at once. China's high-end pharma market after the early 2000s is the reference pattern.

**A top-down story does not license any price.** These two are separable and should be stated separately: "this theme will be large" and "this multiple is payable." Manufacturing is the clean example — it structurally cannot earn services-level returns on equity, because it needs working capital and recurring capex, so a manufacturing thesis has a lower defensible multiple than a services thesis with the same growth rate. Loving a sector and overpaying for it are independent decisions.

Where a theme depends on policy support, the useful question is whether the society *needs* the outcome — employment, regional balance, export capacity. Policy tends to follow structural necessity, though on a timeline nobody can pin down, which is again an argument for position sizing over conviction.

## Cross-sector relative value

Don't only compare a bank to other banks. Put price-to-book against ROE and growth **across** sectors — a lender can look genuinely cheap against a utility or a cement company on that grid. Widening the comparison set is how you find out whether "cheap for its sector" means anything, or whether the whole sector simply re-rated together.

## Is the market expensive? The real-return check

A clean index-level test that's hard to game: long-run real equity returns mean-revert to roughly 6.5–7% (Siegel's work on multi-century US data). Take the market's trailing 10–20 year *nominal* return, subtract inflation over the same period, and compare.

A market that has delivered ~10% real for a decade is not compounding sustainably; it is borrowing from its own future. This works where cross-era P/E comparisons get slippery, because it needs only two numbers and no judgment about what multiple is "normal" now.

## Reading a capex-driven boom

When one sector's capital expenditure reaches a few percent of GDP, the historical bar for sustaining it is very high — railroads and the dotcom-era telecom buildout are the comparison set, and both ended with the capex, not the technology, being wrong.

Keep two questions apart, because they have different probabilities and very different consequences:

1. **Is there a credit event?** Look at which balance sheets are funding the spend and at what the credit market is charging them. Strong balance sheets can absorb a writedown on a bad capex cycle; leveraged pure-plays cannot. Widening spreads on the marginal borrower are the tell, well before anything appears in earnings.
2. **Does a capex slowdown drive a broad slowdown?** If recent growth has been capex-led rather than consumption-led, then a pause propagates — and this is usually the larger and less-priced risk, because it doesn't require anyone to default.

Two more patterns to look for. **Circular financing** — a vendor investing in a customer who then contracts spend that returns to the vendor's ecosystem — inflates apparent demand and obscures what real end revenue is. And **the monetisation assumption**: if a cheaper substitute (an open-source alternative, a new entrant with different economics) can undercut pricing power, the capex may be sound and the return on it still zero.

## Flows are a real force — label them as such

Mandated funds must own something inside their mandate. When a theme inside a sector breaks, money rotates to the least-exposed name in that same sector for capital preservation, not because its earnings improved. This can move a stock substantially with no fundamental change at all.

Recognise it, use it, and **name it as a flow argument** — do not retrofit an earnings story onto it. Two theses that look the same on a chart ("this will re-rate") have completely different kill-conditions depending on whether the driver is flows or fundamentals, and mislabelling it means you'll hold it for the wrong reason and exit at the wrong time.

## Fund size: a hypothesis, not a conclusion

Conventional wisdom says scale kills alpha, and the honest update from people who once believed it firmly is that the evidence is mixed. Size does reduce nimbleness — you can't move a large book quickly. It also funds a deeper research team, and the largest managers have not systematically underperformed over decade-long windows.

Which effect dominates depends on whether the team was actually built. So treat "this fund is too big now" as a testable claim — look at breadth of coverage, cash discipline, and whether the strategy's edge was nimbleness in the first place — rather than as an automatic verdict.

## Temperament

The part that actually separates practitioners, and the hardest to fake:

- **Detachment is a screening criterion.** Someone who agonises over one-month and three-month performance cannot hold a three-year fundamental position; they will exit at the worst moment and call it risk management.
- **Expect unknown unknowns**, and build a book that survives them rather than a forecast that excludes them.
- **Learn from opposite styles.** One mentor's rule: *you don't play a bubble, you'll never make big money.* Another's: never believe a bubble is real. Holding both produces calibration; adopting one produces dogma.
- **History over forecasts.** Turning points arrive without warning — which is the argument against rigidity, and against treating buy-and-hold as the default now that price discovery is fast and the macro regime is less stable than the one that produced that advice.

## Output format

**Constructing a thesis:** the claim in one sentence, then the three-to-four-year math (what has to be true, at what exit multiple), then what would disprove it, then what only the user can check. Put the doubling test result up front — if it fails, say so and stop rather than writing three paragraphs of favourable context first.

**Stress-testing an existing position or view:** state whether the thesis is intact, deferred, or broken, and on what evidence. Then the two or three variables the outcome actually hangs on, separated into controllable and external. Then what you'd need to see to change your mind.

**Sector or market-level question:** growth, then consolidation state, then valuation — in that order, because a fragmenting industry's multiple doesn't matter and a consolidating one's often does.

Match depth to the question. A one-line query gets the relevant test applied, not the whole framework recited.

## Don't manufacture the numbers

This framework runs on specifics — growth rates, ROE, cost of equity, price-to-book, exit multiples, capex as a share of GDP. That creates constant pressure to supply a plausible figure where none was given, and an invented number here doesn't just weaken the analysis, it inverts it: a thesis built on a fabricated growth rate will confidently recommend the opposite of the right thing.

So treat every input as something to be sourced or asked for. When the numbers aren't available, the finding is *"here are the four figures this thesis hangs on, and I don't have them"* — that list is genuinely valuable, because it tells the user exactly what to go pull. Mark any illustrative figure visibly (`[current ROE?]`, `[FY29 EBITDA estimate]`) and say in the preamble that the arithmetic is a worked shape, not a result.

Be equally careful with the qualitative claims that feel safe to assert: market share, who the industry leader is, whether a sector is consolidating, what a management team has executed. These are checkable facts, and a wrong one propagates through every test above.
