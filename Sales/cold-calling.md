# Cold Calling

A complete outbound system: what to prepare before dialing, how to run the first 30 seconds, how to qualify without wasting anyone's time, how to sound, and how to handle whatever comes back. The through-line is that cold calling is a game of questions played one rung at a time — every good move buys you the next 10 seconds.

## When to Use

Use this skill when the user asks about:
- Cold calling or cold outreach strategy, scripts, or openers
- Reaching enterprise decision-makers who don't respond to ads or forms
- Validating a product, offer, or market before investing (research calls)
- Qualifying or disqualifying leads (BANT) on a first call
- Handling objections — price, budget, timing, "send me an email"
- Tonality, delivery, and composure on sales calls
- Booking meetings from outbound; follow-up structure after a yes
- Using cold calls to land a job

## Core Mindset (Everything Else Fails Without This)

1. **Sales is helping, not taking.** Most reluctant sellers (and most bad ones) secretly believe a sale takes money *from* someone. A good sale leaves the prospect smiling, because they received more value than they paid.
2. **The delta is the whole game.** Delta = (value the product gives the client) − (price the client pays). The larger the delta, the easier the sale. Your job on a call is not to persuade — it's to help the prospect *see* the delta. Once they see it, they ask you to sell to them.
3. **Composure is the core skill.** People will be stressed, rude, or angry — often legitimately, because you interrupted their day. You are sitting on gold they can't see yet. Stay calm, apologize where warranted, and keep playing. Converting everyone is not the goal; converting the callable percentage is.
4. **Ruthless curiosity.** Surface answers are worthless. Keep asking "why" until you hit the specific, substantive answer. Most people have never been asked three whys in a row.
5. **Sales is a game of questions.** The one asking questions is directing the conversation. Never end a turn with a statement — a statement leaves the prospect nowhere to go; a question tells them exactly what to say next.

## Three Uses of Cold Calling (Beyond Selling)

**1. Market research before you burn money.** Before building anything, call the actual people who would buy it. A month of structured research calls can save years and huge sums by killing a bad idea early — recordings of real ICPs saying "that's not actually our problem, *this* is" are worth more than any survey. Research rules:
- Don't ask "would you buy this?" — everyone says yes and nobody shows up. Ask the **three W's**: *Why* would you buy it? *What* about it? *Where* are you on a 1–10 scale? Then: "Why aren't you at the top?" (they tell you what they like) and "What would it need to be for you to say 'call me the moment it launches'?" (they tell you what's missing).
- Research the possibilities, not just your premise. Ask *what would you buy*, not only *would you buy this*.

**2. Reaching enterprise.** Small players pitch cafés and restaurants; the money sits with enterprise, and enterprise doesn't fill out your ad forms. The cheapest, most direct route to a CMO or director is calling them — at that scale they're actively hunting for solutions and will often talk openly about their problems. A phone and a number get you into rooms conferences can't.

**3. Getting a job.** Skip HR. Call the manager of the function you want to join (sales manager for sales, marketing manager for marketing), with researched context and real energy. Companies are starved for people who visibly care; a good cold call *is* the interview.

## Pre-Call Homework (Do This Before Anyone Dials)

**Step 1 — Value proposition.** One sentence that drives the whole call: **"I do X through Y to get Z"** — what you do, the process, the outcome. Example shape: "We do organic social media through video production to make you go viral." If the business can't produce this sentence, fix that before calling anyone.

**Step 2 — ICP and SAM model.** Define the ideal customer as a *specific individual*: role/title, seniority, industry, location, income range, where they hang out. The SAM model = build one composite person who embodies all of it.

**Step 3 — Data.** Everybody's number is two clicks away; the question is data quality:
- **Starting out / low budget:** Apollo, Lusha — great entry points, but data can be outdated.
- **Serious / enterprise budgets:** FullEnrich (strong for UK/US) or Apollo's higher tiers — these use a **waterfall method** (cascading multiple data providers) for much fresher numbers. You're paying to not waste dialing time.
- You can also buy lists against your SAM spec from data vendors — thousands of numbers matched to the persona.

**Step 4 — Pre-plan the branches.** After enough calls, prospects only ever say four or five things at each stage. Write down the expected responses and your reply to each. The call becomes an algorithm, not an improvisation (see Call Flow below).

**Step 5 — Pick your open and tone.** Choose an opening statement that fits your authentic tonality (see Tonality), and rehearse it until it doesn't sound like a script.

## The 30-Second Rule and the Ladder

A cold call grants you **30 seconds of grace**. If you don't create intrigue or get a yes inside them, the call is over. The model: every correct move **moves the prospect up a ladder and unlocks ~10 more seconds**. You are never trying to win the whole call at once — only the next rung.

Two absolute rules for the open:
1. **Don't pitch.**
2. **Don't sound like a salesperson.** (No "sir", no "may I have your permission", no last names.)

These rules are specific to cold calls — inbound sales plays by different rules.

## Openers

All openers work if they achieve one thing: **a pattern interrupt** that earns the question "who is this?" — because then *they are asking you to pitch* instead of you pitching at them.

- **Old friend open (default):** First name only, warm familiarity: "Ganesh! How are you doing?" It sounds like someone from school they haven't heard from in years, so they don't hang up — they ask "who's this?" That question is the goal.
- **Honest hard open:** "By the way — this is a cold call. Do I get 30 seconds?" Disarming honesty is itself a pattern interrupt.
- **Novelty opens:** anything unexpected ("If I sing you jingle bells, will you give me 30 seconds?"). Silly is fine; predictable is fatal.

Experiment. There are hundreds of opens; keep whichever one *you* can deliver authentically. Every open leads to only two or three responses: "who is this?", "what is this about?", or "don't call me again" — and you should have a scripted next move for each.

## The Call Flow (Algorithm)

**1. Open** → they ask "who's this?"

**2. Identity + pre-objection handle:** "This is [first name] from [company name] — is this a good time?"
- Say the company name *without explaining it*. Now they must ask "what is [company]?" — again, they're pulling the pitch out of you.
- **"Is this a good time?" is a pre-objection handle:** once they've answered it, they can't later kill the call with "this isn't a good time" — that objection has already been spent. General principle: objections you see repeatedly late in calls should be moved to the front and defused before they can occur.

**3. Value proposition (the pitch, in one line):** the moment they ask "what is this about?" / "what is [company]?", deliver the Step-1 sentence and immediately convert it to a question — the **intent check**: "…Is this something you're looking for?"
- If they said "this is not a good time" instead: cushion and pitch anyway on your way out — "I completely understand. Before I let you go: we do X through Y to get Z — is this something you'd want, or should I make sure nobody from my team ever calls you again?" You always get the one-line pitch in; the framing changes, the content doesn't.

**4. Qualification (BANT).** Interest alone is worthless — the objective is not meetings, it's meetings with people who can buy. Frame it as a favor: "I don't want to waste your time on another call — can I ask a few quick questions to check it even makes sense?" Then:
- **Budget:** "If you were to do this, what investment would you be open to, if it made sense?"
- **Authority:** "Is this something you can decide on? What's the decision-making process?"
- **Need:** "When you say [category], what specifically do you mean? What have you tried?" (their "social media" and yours may be different things)
- **Timeline:** "What's your timeline for deciding?" — this sorts hot vs. warm vs. nurture leads. Pressure on a not-now lead loses them; no pressure on an urgent lead loses them too.

Each BANT answer routes to one of **four outcomes**: **qualify** (book the meeting), **disqualify** (off the list, don't waste either side's time), **educate** (right fit, needs information — keep on the roster), or **realign** (their expectation of budget/timeline is unrealistic — correct it, keep them warm).

Rule of thumb: if you're short on pipeline, don't qualify hard — take the calls for practice. If your calendar is full, your job on cold calls is to *disqualify*.

**5. Close for one of three outcomes — the fallback ladder.** Don't leave until you've collected three no's:
1. **Book a meeting.** If no →
2. **Permission to stay in touch:** "This might not be relevant now — okay if I check in in a few months, and send you the occasional useful material?" These are still ICPs; they may need you in a quarter. If no →
3. **A reference:** "Who do you think this *would* be relevant for?" A referred call starts warm: "…[Name] told me you're the person I should be speaking to."

**Booking mechanics:**
- **Assumption-based > permission-based.** Not "would you like to get on a call?" but "I have two blocks open — Thursday or Friday, which works for a 15-minute call?" The question is never *whether*, only *when*. Always offer two concrete slots (they can still counter with their own — fine).
- After the whole conversation has been yes/no questions, the closing question inherits the momentum: by then it's just "Thursday or Friday?"
- **Cardinal sin:** letting them go before the invite is confirmed *visible on their calendar*, and before telling them you'll call the morning of the meeting to reconfirm. A booked meeting that no-shows is worth nothing.

## Tonality

Tonality is ~90% of delivery — people forget your words and remember the feeling, and the feeling comes from tone. You can't fake it long-term; you need the mindset (sales is helping) underneath it.

**Four pillars:** **Rhythm** (the flow and musicality of the words — think of the most charming person you know), **Pitch** (deep vs. shrill — not the same as volume), **Volume** (soft vs. loud), **Pause** (the most important: intensity lives in the spaces, not the words).

**Six core tonalities** — and when each one earns its keep:

| # | Tonality | How | When |
|---|----------|-----|------|
| 1 | Friendly & inviting | Medium pitch, steady volume, genuine smile | Opens, rapport, general connection |
| 2 | Calm & reassuring | Lower pitch, soft volume, slow pace | Discovery and qualification — pain doesn't come out for a cheerful tone; it comes out for a therapist |
| 3 | Confident & assertive | Steady, strong (not loud), emphasis on key words, upright posture | Stating the price, answering objections. A submissive price delivery kills deals — the buyer is scanning for confidence that you can deliver |
| 4 | Enthusiastic | Higher pitch, louder, faster pace, emphasized words | Creating urgency (limited slots, launches, EOI-style scarcity) and mirroring excitement about the prospect's own work |
| 5 | Empathetic | Low pitch, slow pace, gentle | Responding to pain surfaced in discovery — "it's tough, I get it, we've all been there" — this is what buys deeper trust |
| 6 | Persuasive & compelling | Start low and slow (reflective), build to higher and faster (assertive), hard pause after key statements | Objection handling — open reflective so they know you listened, land assertive so the answer carries, pause so it sinks in |

**Authenticity rule:** don't prescribe one tone as best. Everyone has one or two native tonalities; forcing an alien one sounds off and reads as fake. Identify the native tone, use it as home base, and train the others deliberately. Hiring implication: match the salesperson's native tone to the offering — friendly for cars, calm for support, assertive for high-ticket.

## Objection Handling

**The framework: agree → ask → isolate → realign/educate.**

1. **Never reject the premise.** The instant you say "no, it's not expensive," it's one-against-one and a wall goes up — nothing you say after is heard. Open with a **cushion phrase**: "I completely understand," "That's completely fair," "I get where you're coming from." Agreeing is not submitting; it lowers their guard so it becomes *you and the client versus the problem*.
2. **Ask to the core.** "Can I ask why you feel it's expensive?" Then keep going — compared to what? What do you get for that price? Why didn't you go with them? Most stated objections aren't the real one; your job is objection *discovery* before objection handling.
3. **Isolate before you solve.** "Suppose I could fix the price — is price the *only* thing stopping you from moving forward today?" If yes, solve it and close. If no, ask what else — collect the full list, then handle one at a time. Never solve an objection that can be immediately replaced by a fresh one.
4. **Realign and educate.** Once you know the real belief ("all editors are the same"), walk them to the distinction with questions, not assertions: "Is an editor who's never produced content at scale the same as one with consistent millions of views?" Let them say it.

**Useful distinctions and tools:**
- **Buying objections vs. real objections.** "Can we do something about the price?" *after* intent is established isn't resistance — the decision is already made and they want a little support (or a win). Stay calm, keep it light, get to the other side. (Deadpan option: "100% — I can make it more expensive.")
- **"No budget" splits in two.** *No access to capital* (genuinely broke) → disqualify politely. *Allocation* (money exists, committed elsewhere or not prioritized) → realign and educate; that's a value-delta problem, not a money problem.
- **The 1–10 scale (via Alex Hormozi).** "On a scale where one end is 'take my money now' and the other is 'never call me again' — where are you?" Then: "Why not at the bottom?" (they re-sell themselves on everything they like) and "What would it take to get you to the top?" (they hand you the exact gap). If the gap is small, fix it and close.

## Quick Reference

```
HOMEWORK: value prop (X through Y to get Z) → ICP/SAM → data (Apollo→FullEnrich)
          → pre-script the 4-5 possible responses per stage → pick open + tone

CALL:     pattern-interrupt open → "who's this?"
          → name + company + "is this a good time?" (pre-objection handle)
          → they ask what it is → one-line value prop → "is this something you're looking for?"
          → BANT (budget / authority / need / timeline)
          → qualify | disqualify | educate | realign
          → meeting (2 time slots, assumption close)
            ↳ no? permission to stay in touch
              ↳ no? ask for a reference
          → confirm invite on their calendar + morning-of reminder call

OBJECTION: cushion ("I completely understand") → ask why to the core
           → isolate ("if I fix this, do we move forward today?")
           → realign/educate with questions → assertive answer + pause
```
